Engineering excellence is a business term

2026-10-01

Engineering excellence is one of those phrases that seems to exist almost exclusively inside businesses. You don't usually hear someone talking about engineering excellence in open-source or a side project. You just build things in the best way you can.

That's probably because "engineering excellence" is a way of talking about quality in a business context. And in a business, everything needs a certain amount of formality before it gets taken seriously. Calling something "engineering excellence" gives quality a name that catches the eye of non-techies. It's like taking something mundane such as coffee and giving it a fancy name like Mocca Latte.

Quality isn't absolute

I used to think quality was non-negotiable, but that's wrong, we negotiate quality all the time, even with ourselves. When you are building a side project you are constantly negotiating quality: Do I write tests now or later? Do I include the most secure practices in the first release or do I wait for v2? Do I improve the data model, knowing that it will lead to a huge refactor, or do I accept it the way it is?

There are certainly things that I would consider non-negotiable, though. If I know that software I'm about to release exposes users' personal information, for example, I'm not going to be comfortable deploying it just because someone from the business told me to. But most engineering decisions are not as black-or-white like that. Most of the time we're negotiating quality in much more subtle ways.

And that's uncomfortable if you think of quality as a binary property: either something has quality or it doesn't. In reality, quality is a spectrum, and where you place yourself on that spectrum depends heavily on context. "Good quality" doesn't mean the same for a side project you build for yourself, or for a small startup that might not exist in six months, or an established company with millions of users. The same engineering decision can therefore be perfectly reasonable in one context and completely unreasonable in another.

Engineering is a craft

I've heard many times that programming is an art. I, personally, disagree with that statement. I understand why people arrive at that conclusion, though, after all there is a component of creativity involved in programming. But, somewhat paradoxically, I do think programming is a craft. There should be a certain solemnity about the work we do. We should take it seriously, we should care about doing it well, understand the consequences of our decisions, and take responsibility for the software we put into the world.

That doesn't mean every decision needs to hyper-optimize for technical quality or that we should fight for our ideas until the last consequences. It's even more difficult than that: It means we should understand the tradeoffs well enough to explain them to non-technical people.

Knowing is half the battle, communicating is the other half

One of the hardest things for engineers to accept is that being technically right is a great starting point, but it isn't enough.

I've had plenty of conversations with non-technical people where I've argued for something and failed to convince them. My instinct used to be to blame the other person for not understanding the technical issue. But if you're talking to someone who is capable of reasoning about the problem, there are really only two possibilities: you didn't explain it properly, or you don't have a sufficiently good argument.

Having the technical knowledge means that you can see things that other people can't necessarily see. Your job is therefore partly to fill in those gaps. Don't assume that someone outside engineering understands the consequences of a particular technical decision. Explain the problem in terms they can understand. Explain what happens if you don't address it. Explain what you're proposing and why.

This is a critical part of our job that nobody teaches us. We learn to communicate with other engineers, but we also need to communicate with people who don't have our technical background.

Connect quality to a problem

If you can't connect a quality improvement to some kind of business value, it's very difficult to make a convincing case for it. That doesn't mean everything has to be reduced to a precise number of euros or dollars. Not every engineering decision has an immediately measurable financial return. But there should be at the very least a problem behind the recommendation.

For example, "this is a problem we have today, and this is how the proposed change solves it". Or "this is a problem we're likely to encounter if we continue down this path, and this is how we can prevent it".That's a much stronger argument than simply saying, "This isn't good engineering." Imagine taking your car to the mechanic and they propose to change the car's transmission because the one you have is not the one that model originally shipped with. Your first question would be "so?", and the second: "how's that a problem?"

We're the experts, not the owners

Ultimately, this is one of the most uncomfortable truths for programmers in the industry: the business gets to decide whether that investment is worth making. Yes, technical decisions are ultimately made by non-technical people. That hurts, I know.

We're the experts in our domain. We have years of experience, we've seen systems fail in particular ways, and we often have a pretty good idea of what is going to happen if a decision is made. But expertise doesn't give us ownership over the decision. Our role is to put our expertise on the table: this is what I've seen before, this is the problem I think we're going to have, and this is what I recommend doing about it. Then someone else may decide that the cost isn't worth it.

At that point, our job is to decide whether we're willing to work within those constraints. If the decisions being made fundamentally conflict with our own values, perhaps that isn't the right place for us. But we don't get to be offended simply because our recommendation wasn't accepted.

Engineering excellence isn't an absolute standard that overrides every other consideration. It's quality applied within a business, but it's the business who decides how much quality they can afford.
