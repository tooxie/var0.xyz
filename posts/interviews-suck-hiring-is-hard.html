Interviews suck, hiring is hard

2026-09-09

Interviews suck, I know. I don't think that's a hot take. What's more interesting is that even though we all seem to agree in that they suck, we keep using them as the main and only evaluation tool essentially unchanged for decades.

The problem isn't simply that technical interviews are stressful or that nobody enjoys solving programming puzzles under a timer. The deeper problem is that we often end up evaluating people on whatever is easiest for us to measure rather than what we actually need them to do.

What are you actually hiring for?

I once interviewed for a senior position where the company was looking for someone to join a team of junior developers and guide them. The interview was largely programming exercises, which is not an inherently unreasonable way to evaluate a programmer but it made me wonder what the company was actually hiring for.

If you need someone to lead a team, the important questions aren't necessarily whether they can solve a programming puzzle quickly. Can they help other developers grow? Can they communicate clearly? Can they recognise when a seemingly simple task is actually complicated? Can they give useful estimates? Can they keep people moving when the requirements aren't perfectly clear?

Those are very different skills.

We test what we know how to test

I think this is one of the fundamental problems with designing interviews. If you're a programmer interviewing another programmer, you know how to evaluate programming. Give them some code and see what they do with it. But evaluating communication is harder. Evaluating teamwork is harder. Evaluating someone's ability to navigate ambiguity is harder. Evaluating whether they will make good technical decisions six months from now is much harder.

So we gravitate toward the things we can measure.

It's the same trap we fall into everywhere else in engineering: the thing that's easiest to measure slowly becomes the thing we optimise for. And eventually you end up with a process that's very good at selecting people who are good at interviews.

Interviewing is its own skill

There is another uncomfortable problem: being good at interviews and being good at the job are not the same thing. Interviewing is a strange, artificial activity. You're expected to perform while somebody you've never met analyzes your every move in realtime, your decisions, what you say, even what you don't. You're given unfamiliar problems, often with limited context and an artificial time constraint.

Most jobs aren't like that, you work on familiar (even repetitive, monotonous) tasks. At work, you have access to documentation. You can search the internet. You can talk to colleagues. You can go away, think about a problem and come back to it tomorrow. You can learn something you don't know.

And most importantly, if you ever decide to write your own sorting algorithm, someone would question your sanity.

There is no perfect interview

So the solution is obvious: just design better interviews. Right? Well... that's incredibly difficult.

What does a good interview for a good team player look like? How do you distinguish someone who communicates well from someone who has simply learned how to give good interview answers?

Some people would rather spend a couple of hours working through an interview and be done with it. Others would prefer a take-home assignment that they can complete at their own pace. Someone with children or other responsibilities may have very different constraints from someone who can dedicate an entire evening to an interview process.

You may think that giving the candidate the option to choose which kind of interview they prefer would be the solution, but keep in mind that an in-person interview and a take-home assignment evaluate fundamentally different things. Would you say that doing leetcode is technically equivalent to building an API (possibly with its own Dockerfile or terraform code)?

Fairness is difficult

This is what makes the whole process so frustrating. We're trying to make a consequential decision about a person using a tiny, artificial sample of their abilities. At the same time, we want the process to be fair and comparable between candidates.

One person might be brilliant at thinking aloud while another needs some quiet time to work through a problem. One might thrive in a live coding session while another produces excellent work given a day to think. Someone might be nervous because the interview matters enormously to them, while someone else happens to be unusually comfortable in this particular environment.

How much of that should count? I don't really have an answer.

And yet, it's what we have

Despite all of this we don't have a better alternative. Hiring is an inherently difficult problem. You need to make a decision with incomplete information, often about someone you've never worked with before. There are better and worse ways of doing it, but there is no interview that can perfectly predict how someone will perform in a job. Some people will fall through the cracks.

Some excellent candidates will have a bad interview. Some people who interview exceptionally well won't work out. That's the uncomfortable trade-off we're dealing with. If I were designing an interview process, one thing I'd at least consider is giving candidates some choice in how they demonstrate their abilities. Because remember that we all have different strengths.

In a team of backend engineers one could excel at understanding the product, another at security, another one could be the best at diffusing conflict within the team and smoothing communication. All those skills are invaluable and complementary. If you overfit for one skill such as leetcode you will be missing out on a rich and diverse set of skills.

Ultimately, though, I don't have a grand solution to the interview problem.

My suggestion, if you are in the position of running an interview, make sure that for every question you ask, you have an answer to this question: What am I looking to learn from the candidate with this question? And if the answer is "if they know the answer" then you are in the wrong path.

The important thing is to remember what we're actually trying to measure: Not someone's ability to interview, but whether they're likely to be good at the job.
