Syrinx: A censorship-resistant communications platform

2026-10-20

The idea of Syrinx was born many years ago when I started wondering if it would be possible to build a system that couldn't be censored. What if you could come back up as quickly as you can spin up a new server, in the case of a hostile take over? Turns out that by having your data cryptographically signed and distributed among the network members you don't have to worry about backups any more.

This idea became even more relevant in the last year or so when the latest (and the most consequential) attack on our privacy began spreading through different countries in the form of age verification laws.

The State of Online Privacy

Online rights have been slowly stripped away, but to be fair they haven't been taken away as much as we have given them up in exchange for convenience. We provide constantly our location, every message we send, every photo we take, every email we write, every song we listen, every article we read, every video we watch. All of our information is constantly harvested, processed, analyzed, and sold. Sometimes they want to sell us stuff, sometimes they want to know our immigration status, sometimes they want to know our political affiliation. We have no control over any of this.

And now that they have all that information we would be forced to attach an ID card to it, to make sure that you can't escape the panopticon.

The 57005 Internet?

I'm sure you've heard about the 57005 Internet theory, which states that most of the Internet traffic is just bots talking to each other. Now exacerbated by most content being AI-generated. I don't know if this is true, but I don't care really, because google is not the Internet, instagram is not the Internet, twitter is not the Internet, and reddit is not the Internet.

The Internet is decentralized by design. The fact that most of the traffic is handled by a handful of corporations is an anomaly, this is not how it was supposed to work.

The Proof of Concept

Syrinx is an experiment, a PoC. I wanted to see if I was able to come up with a communications platform that:

  • Was private.
  • Could survive a takedown.
  • Was easy to run.
    • And performed reasonably well in modest hardware.
  • Could federate with other communities.
  • Gave admins certain tools of moderation.

The application stores all data in the devices of its members, the server meanwhile acts as a torrent tracker, i.e. it knows "who has what" but it doesn't know what the content is. So if I want post X it knows who has it, not what it's written there. A client encrypts the content, sends it to the server, which in turn delivers it to the user who requested it. The content gets decrypted, validated, and stored.

I was hesitant to call Syrinx "P2P" so in the documentation I called it P2P-ish. This is because establishing direct connections between peers across NAT'ted routers is extremely unreliable, so the server acts as a relay in between the peers.

Since the database is distributed among the peers a hostile take-over of the server does not yield any useful information besides usernames (emails and bios are optional.) This allows the system to become operational again quickly as long as users export their data from the old app and import it into the new system.

Moderation is important for a community to make sure that communication remains safe, respectful, and overall aligned with the community-established guidelines. This power given to a few people is balanced by publishing every action they take to a cryptographically-signed public ledger that every member can read. That makes them accountable and unable to exercise power in darkness.

This goes hand-in-hand with the community being invite-only. That instills a sense of responsibility on the members when inviting other people, knowing that their actions reflect on you as well, and bringing in members that could disrupt the peace could have consequences on your own account.

Just because I don't agree with what you say does not mean that you don't have the right to say it. Members who disagree on how the community is run could easily start their own. That's why it's important that the software is easy to run at home, in whatever piece of hardware you have lying around. This allows for small communities to flourish that can then federate with each other in a sort of mesh.

A Message to You, Rudy

This is my message to the hacker community out there: We have the means to reclaim the Internet. We don't need to depend on corporations for hosting or compute power, that's not how the Internet was designed. In fact, the hyper-centralized Internet that we have today is an anomaly.

As I always say, Software Engineering is the art of managing trade-offs, and Syrinx is no exception. There are big trade-offs when it comes to decentralized services. Yet, I believe that rather than losing something, these limitations give the system very particular and unique traits that I have come to appreciate.

Let me go into each trade-off one by one and explain how they, in my opinion, improve the user experience.

Content-Type: text/plain

Syrinx is text-only and posts limited to 140 characters. This is not because it's impossible to have P2P multimedia, but because solving it for text was big enough of a problem to tackle in a PoC. On top of that my phone for example reports that the app is provided with a quota of 10G of storage even if I don't have 10G phisically available in my device. On the client we have to be very conservative when it comes to storage, developers should consume it carefully and responsibly.

That said, this system is inspired by the golden days of Twitter, when 140 characters, far from limiting expresssion, fostered creativity. I would call this decision "partly technical, but mostly intentional", since I could have started with a much bigger limit with no noticeable impact on storage. Just to give you an idea, 90% of a post's size is consumed by its envelope, namely the cryptographic signatures.

Text-only means that no profile pictures are allowed either. I have discovered that this has some very interesting security properties. Since the username is just free text you could try to impersonate a user by using similar-looking characters or maybe even invisible characters. However, since you can't have an arbitrary profile picture (your avatar will be auto-generated from the server-generated user ID, AKA an identicon) you will get a distinct avatar, giving both accounts unique visual characteristics that prevent impersonation.

Content Travels at Angular Speed

As we already established the server does not hold any content, therefore peers have to be online to deliver it; if there's nobody online at the time you request the content, you can't access it. This is inspired by the BBS of the early days of the Internet, in which a message could take days to reach the recipient because servers would only sync once a day, at night.

In Syrinx this means that a user you follow who is located on your antipode (i.e. on the exact opposite side of earth) may be sleeping when you try to access it. In order for you to get it they should deliver it to someone that's closer to your timezone while they are awake, and then that user could act as your provider.

I really love this concept that delivery not only doesn't have to be immediate, but that content could literally take hours or even days travelling across the earth before it can reach you.

The Walled Web

I said earlier that we have all the tools to reclaim the Internet. And I do believe that. However, the entrypoints are very well policed.

There are just a handful of browsers available and all of them, directly or indirectly, controlled by corporations. The view is even more dire in the mobile world, where the whole ecosystem is built and maintained by corporations. They decide who gets to play in a game where they not only set the rules, but also change them whenever they feel like.

Progressive Web Apps are a tiny crack in that wall, but with many limitations (with respect to native apps) many of them undocumented or disguised as bugs. It's not uncommon to hit features that are supposed to work according to the docs, but haven't for years. And the incentives for fixing them are not really there either.

Still, we can work with what we have. For now I've decided to leave certain features out, such as notifications. I want the user to come to the app when they decide, not the app to scream for attention. I do see the value in getting notified of important events, but what "important" means should be defined by the user.

Data Migration

This is the one problem I have not been able to solve yet. In a centralized environment you would have a domain acting as the server's identity. If I query that domain I can be reasonably certain that I'm reaching the actual service.

In a decentralized world there's no single authority to tell me who is who, I depend on someone (that I trust) giving me the public key of the server over a secure channel to be able to verify their identity. Now imagine that I want to migrate my user from one community to another.

If these 2 communities are federated (meaning that they have already verified each other's identities) the new home could validate that the data was signed by the old server and accept it. But what if they don't know about each other? How can I be sure that the user didn't craft a set of signatures themselves, pretending to be server signatures?

And what if the original community is permanently down? A user who doesn't want to lose their posts decides to migrate their identity to a new server. Now even if the user brings the old server's public key along there's no server to ask whether they even know about this user. If this user claims they they wrote a post in 2019 about a new pandemic coming up, shall we trust them? If you do, you (the server) are vouching for the post's validity.

It's all complicated enough already that we are moving just public posts between servers, now add private chats too in the mix. It's a mess. This is, in my opinion, the Achilles' heel of this solution, and one that I still have no satisfactory answer for. To be fair, no centralized, proprietary service allows you to migrate your data and nobody seems to have a problem with that (you can export it at best, but nobody will let you import it.)

So What's Next?

I don't know. I proved what I set out to prove: Whether it was possible to have a federated social network that could withstand censorship while providing a basic set of tools for moderation. And I'm very satisfied with the result. What happens next is up to the community.

Let me know if you find it useful, I would appreciate any kind of feedback.

Thanks for reading.
